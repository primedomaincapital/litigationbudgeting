AI Is Rewriting the Litigation Budget — and Nobody Agrees on Who Keeps the Savings

A general counsel opens the quarterly legal bill and finds the same hours she paid for last year, at a higher rate, for work her own team now does with an AI tool in an afternoon. She calls the firm. The firm explains that its AI licenses cost money too. Neither side is lying. That is the problem.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsNovember 9, 2026

The numbers in Thomson Reuters' Law Firm Rates Report 2027 are startling in their plainness. The average standard rate for U.S. firms is $800 per hour, dropping to $720 after initial discounts are applied. Worked rates for the Am Law 100 grew 9.7 percent in the year to June 2026, against PCE inflation of 3.1 percent. All segments grew 7.1 percent. The prices of legal services have been surging for years — a shift, the report notes, that did not begin with the arrival of ChatGPT in late 2022. And 90 percent of legal dollars still flow through hourly billing, according to the Thomson Reuters Institute and Georgetown Law's 2026 Report on the State of the U.S. Legal Market.

That is the setting for the strangest bargain in the legal economy. The tasks that fill a junior associate's day — document review, first-draft research, diligence — are precisely the tasks generative AI performs best. Thomson Reuters' 2026 Future of Professionals Report, surveying 1,816 professionals in 62 countries, found 74 percent use AI several times a week. Harvey, the legal AI company, raised $550 million at a $15.5 billion valuation and says it serves 80 percent of the Am Law 100. Legora, founded in Stockholm in 2023, has rolled out firmwide at Linklaters. Technology spending grew 9.7 percent in 2025, and knowledge management tools grew 10.5 percent — the fastest real growth likely ever experienced in the legal industry, per the State of the U.S. Legal Market report.

The meter is running

The new cost is not the subscription. It is the usage. At ILTACON 2026, firms started doing the arithmetic in public. The per-seat subscription is the number firms budgeted for; it is increasingly not the number they pay. The AI tool count, as tracked by Legaltech Hub, is growing better than 30 percent year over year and now runs past 1,100 companies. Stacks of programs per firm, each with its own consumption meter, increasingly running agents that spend tokens on work no human is directly watching. Tim Fox, Chief Data and AI Officer at Ogletree Deakins, said he has spent years in legal technology and has never seen the market move like this. Most firms, he said, are settling on AI costing roughly 1 percent of revenue. His own firm is not there yet.

Littler knowledge management counsel Johnny Wong put the arithmetic in the plainest terms: firms are investing millions in the technology. He described checking a consumption bill and finding that a single PowerPoint had cost him $2. The examples nobody volunteers are the memo regenerated nine times when the second version was already fine, the frontier model summarizing a two-paragraph email, the agent that ran all night against a task nobody opened in the morning. Some major companies have already switched AI services off entirely, because usage was not producing revenue.

The client sees none of it

Here is the figure that should worry every general counsel. In a 2025 Association of Corporate Counsel and Everlaw survey of 657 in-house professionals across 30 countries, nearly 60 percent had seen no noticeable savings from outside counsel's use of generative AI. Of that group, 58 percent said their firms had not adjusted pricing to reflect the efficiencies. Sixty-one percent of all respondents said they were likely to push for pricing changes. A November 2025 survey found 72 percent of U.S. firms offer alternative fee arrangements, but only 1.9 percent said pricing flexibility was a significant factor in winning or retaining clients — because with worked rates growing a record 7.3 percent in 2025, firms can afford to wait.

The clients are noticing anyway. Am Law 200 revenue grew 12.4 percent in the first half of 2026, and Wells Fargo reports that clients are scrutinizing bills more closely and paying more slowly. Twenty-six percent of in-house teams expect to cut spending on law firms in 2026. Morgan Stanley, Citi, and Goldman Sachs are pressing their firms for AI-related fee cuts. First-year hiring at the largest U.S. firms fell 7.5 percent in 2025, the first decline since 2011 — the firms are quietly restructuring the workforce the AI is replacing, while charging the client as if nothing changed.

The counterargument: the firms have a point too

The firms' defense is not frivolous. Thomson Reuters' rates report finds AI is currently boosting law firm profitability primarily by generating new legal demand rather than improving internal efficiency. New regulation, new compliance work, new disputes — the AI era creates legal work faster than it automates it. The firm that invests millions in tools, training, and risk management is entitled to a return, and the client that demands the savings while keeping the risk with the firm is asking for the upside without the exposure.

There is also a real quality question. An AI-drafted brief still needs a lawyer to check it, and the checking is skilled work. The February 2026 market shock, when Anthropic's legal automation push sent Thomson Reuters shares down 18 percent in a day, showed how fast the ground moves — and how fast a firm can be caught holding yesterday's model. The $1.5 billion Bartz settlement, in which Anthropic paid authors over training data, showed the liability side of the same coin.

What the budget looks like next

The litigation budget of 2027 will have a line item that did not exist in 2024: AI consumption, metered by the token, alongside the hourly rate it was supposed to replace. The general counsel who manages it well will ask three questions. What did the AI actually do on this matter, in hours saved? What did the consumption cost? And what part of the savings shows up in my bill? The firms that answer those questions clearly will keep the work. The firms that answer with a higher hourly rate will discover what 26 percent of in-house teams have already decided: the budget is not growing, and the work will go to whoever proves the machine pays for itself.

The billable hour survived the typewriter, the word processor, and the internet. Whether it survives the meter is the open question of the next three years, and the answer will be written in budgets, not in conference keynotes.
