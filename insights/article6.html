The Matter Budget That Held: What Accurate Litigation Forecasts Have in Common

Only about 20 percent of matters stay within budget. The ones that do share the same forecasting practices — and none of them is a better guess.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsNovember 20, 2026

The case settled in March, eighteen months after it was filed, for $2.1 million — and the legal bill came to $412,000, against a budget of $400,000. Nobody celebrated. Nobody complained either. The general counsel filed the variance report, noted the 3 percent overrun, and moved on to the next matter. Three doors down, a different case settled the same month for $1.8 million, against a budget of $900,000, and the bill came to $1.7 million. The second case was not more complex. It was not more important. It was budgeted by a different method, and the method was the whole story.

Litigation budgets fail in predictable ways, and the matters that come in on budget share a small set of forecasting practices that the ones that blow past it do not. The pattern is visible in the survey data: in a Gartner survey of 200 in-house and law firm lawyers, legal matters stayed within planned budgets only about 20 percent of the time. The Association of Corporate Counsel's surveys show the demand side of the same fact — 76 percent of chief legal officers named controlling outside legal spend a top operational priority in 2024, and the 2025 update kept it there. The gap between those two numbers is the market for better forecasting, and the firms and legal departments that close it do the same things.

The first is phase-based budgeting, and it is the practice that separates a forecast from a guess. Instead of a single number for the life of the matter, the budget is broken into phases — investigation and early case assessment, pleadings, discovery, motions, trial preparation, trial — with a number attached to each and a checkpoint at the boundary between them. The Association of Corporate Counsel's CLO survey work finds 78 percent of chief legal officers use formal outside counsel guidelines that mandate phase-by-phase budgets for matters above $50,000, and the guidelines are typically revisited at least every 18 months. The phase structure does two things a single number cannot: it tells the client where the money went when the total moves, and it gives both sides a moment to stop and re-decide before the next phase begins.

Early case assessment: the thirty-to-sixty-day rule

The second practice is early case assessment, done on a deadline. The guidance from Gibson Dunn and Practical Law is to complete the assessment within 30 to 60 days of learning of a potential lawsuit — early enough that the facts still shape the budget, late enough that the assessment is based on evidence rather than anxiety. The assessment answers four questions: what is the exposure, what are the realistic outcomes, what will each phase cost, and at what point does settlement become cheaper than the next phase? A budget built before those questions are answered is a number attached to a hope. A budget built after them is a plan.

The matters that hold together also share a discipline around settlement checkpoints. The budget names the points at which the client and the firm will pause and ask whether the case is still worth fighting — after discovery, after dispositive motions, before trial — and prices the settlement range at each point. Burford Capital, the litigation funder, has reviewed more than 12,000 cases in the course of its diligence, and the pattern its underwriters see is consistent: the cases that destroy budgets are the ones where nobody priced the exit in advance, so the decision to keep fighting is made by inertia rather than arithmetic.

The third practice is the least glamorous and the most predictive: the budget is written down, shared, and tracked monthly against actuals, with variances explained in writing. The Association of Corporate Counsel publishes a sample outside counsel budget with columns for milestones, estimated budgets, revised estimates, actual billed amounts, and reasons for variance — a format that exists precisely because most budgets are tracked in none of those columns. A survey by the Association of Corporate Counsel found in-house counsel required budgets 67.4 percent of the time in litigation matters, against 46 percent in transactions, which means a third of litigation matters still proceed without a required budget at all.

The fourth practice is the after-action review, and it is the one almost nobody does. The Association of Corporate Counsel's Covenant with Counsel — the value-challenge compact between clients and firms — asks both sides to conduct after-action reviews at the end of each matter, to evaluate performance fairly and regularly, and to use the review to improve the next forecast. A legal department that reviews its budgets only when they are blown has no data on why the accurate ones were accurate. A department that reviews every matter, win or lose, builds a record of which phases run long, which firms estimate honestly, and which kinds of cases defeat every forecast. That record is the forecast. Without it, each matter starts from zero, and the same surprises recur under different captions.

The counterargument: some cases cannot be budgeted

The honest objection is that litigation is genuinely uncertain, and no phase structure removes that. A case can acquire a new defendant, a new claim, a hostile judge, or a document set ten times the size anyone expected. The United Kingdom's Association of Costs Lawyers surveyed costs lawyers and found only 5 percent said solicitors always stick to budgets; 67 percent said they sometimes go over, and 26 percent said they always do. Those numbers describe a real feature of litigation, not a failure of character — and the firms that budget best do not claim to predict the unpredictable. They claim to price the predictable accurately and to flag the unpredictable early, so the client is choosing to spend more rather than discovering it.

There is also a cost to the budgeting process itself. Devising a budget can take days or weeks, and a budget that spans the life of a case and identifies every major milestone is expensive to build and expensive to maintain. The firms that complain about budgets have a point: a client that demands a detailed budget and then never reviews it is imposing cost without using the product. The answer is not to stop budgeting. It is to budget at the level of detail the client will actually read — phases and checkpoints, not line items — and to revisit the numbers when the facts change, not when the quarter ends.

What the accurate forecasts have in common

Put the practices together and the pattern is plain. The matters that come in on budget start with an assessment inside sixty days, break the work into phases with a number and a checkpoint each, name the settlement exits in advance, and track actuals against the plan every month with written explanations for variance. The matters that blow past the budget start with a single number, skip the assessment, and discover the overrun in the invoice. The difference is not talent. It is process, and process is the one part of litigation a general counsel can control before the case begins.

The economics reward the discipline. Outside counsel now accounts for 47 percent of total legal spend, up from 45 percent, according to the Association of Corporate Counsel's 2026 benchmarking survey of 576 law departments in 45 countries — the largest single line in the legal budget, and the one most exposed to forecasting error. Worked rates for the Am Law 100 grew 8.0 percent in 2024, a 20-year high, according to Thomson Reuters' State of the U.S. Legal Market report, which means every forecasting error now costs more than it did a year ago. A budget that holds is not an administrative nicety. At current rates, it is the difference between a legal department that controls its largest expense and one that reports it.
