Litigation Budgeting: How GCs Forecast the Unforecastable

Litigation budgeting best practices for 2026: phase-based budgets, ACC legal operations frameworks, variance control, and the tools GCs actually use today.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsOctober 17, 2026

Every general counsel carries a number they never say out loud: what their worst case will actually cost. The litigation budget is the closest they get to saying it. It is also, in the words of more than one legal operations director, a work of speculative fiction that both parties pretend to believe.

The pretense matters. Matters that arrive with a budget hit their targets at rates that unstructured matters simply don't. The Association of Corporate Counsel's survey data has long shown that departments using disciplined budgeting practices — alternative fees among them — have a better than 90% chance of landing within 5% of their established budgets. The budget isn't a prediction. It's a control mechanism.

"A budget is a contract with reality, negotiated with counsel who never agreed to it." That is the wry truth at the center of the discipline. Here's how the best departments make it hold.

Phase-Based Budgets: The UTBMS Discipline

Serious litigation budgeting starts with a shared language. The Uniform Task-Based Management System — the ABA's set of phase and task codes, maintained with Thomson Reuters — divides litigation into phases: case assessment and strategy, fact investigation, discovery, motions, trial preparation, trial, and appeal. Each phase gets its own estimate, its own assumptions, and its own trigger for re-budgeting when assumptions break.

The phase structure does two things spreadsheets alone cannot. First, it forces counsel to price the matter in pieces small enough to argue about. A $2 million "all-in" estimate is unfalsifiable until the invoice arrives. A $180,000 discovery phase with stated assumptions — custodians, volume, review rate — can be tested against reality in month three. Second, it creates natural checkpoints. When a phase overruns by more than 10 to 15%, the engagement letter should require counsel to flag it and re-forecast before continuing. The departments with the tightest variance enforce this as a contractual term, not a courtesy.

The ACC's Legal Operations framework treats matter management — including budgeting — as one of its core functional competencies for a reason. Budgeting is where legal operations earns its keep: turning narrative estimates into structured, trackable commitments.

Budget vs. Actual: Closing the 20% Gap

Industry practice treats a variance of around 20% as the line where a budget stops being a budget and becomes an aspiration. Yet most departments track budget-to-actual on a rhythm too slow to catch the drift — quarterly reviews of matters that burn cash monthly. The best-run departments run monthly variance reports out of their e-billing platforms: Legal Tracker, CounselLink, Brightflag, SimpleLegal. The platform matters less than the cadence. A budget reviewed monthly is a management tool. A budget reviewed quarterly is an autopsy.

The variance report should answer three questions, in order: which phases are over budget, why, and what changes going forward. "Discovery ran long" is not an answer. "Discovery ran long because opposing counsel produced 400,000 documents against an assumed 150,000, and the review rate held at $85 per hour" is an answer — and it feeds directly into the re-forecast for the motions phase, which now inherits a larger record.

One discipline worth stealing from the best legal ops teams: require outside counsel to submit the budget in the same phase structure as the invoice codes. If the budget says "Phase L300: Discovery" and the invoices say "L300," variance analysis is arithmetic. If the invoices say "legal services rendered," it is archaeology.

The Estimate Conversation: Getting Numbers You Can Use

The failure mode of most litigation budgets isn't arithmetic — it's anchoring. Counsel submits a low number to win the work, then manages the overrun. The GC who wants usable budgets has to engineer against this.

Three techniques work. First, demand the assumptions, not just the number: hours by timekeeper level, review volumes, motion practice scope, trial length. Assumptions are where padding hides and where reality can be negotiated. Second, use portfolio data. A department that has budgeted forty employment cases knows what employment cases cost; the e-billing platform is sitting on the answer, phase by phase. Feed it back into the next estimate. Third, tie the budget to the fee structure. A capped fee per phase — 12% of departments now use capped fees in litigation, per the ACC's 2025 benchmarking — converts the estimate from a guess into a commitment. The firm that priced the cap owns the variance.

None of this makes litigation predictable. Opposing counsel will not cooperate with your budget, judges will not respect your phases, and the case you thought would settle in month four will try itself in month fourteen. But the departments that budget in phases, track variance monthly, and tie estimates to fee commitments don't forecast the unforecastable. They manage it — which, in the end, is the only thing a budget ever promised.
