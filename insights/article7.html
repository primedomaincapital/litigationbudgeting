Discovery's Share of the Budget: What eDiscovery Really Costs in 2026

Processing is priced by the gigabyte, hosting by the gigabyte per month, and review by the document. Three units, one invoice — and the largest share is the one clients budget for last.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsNovember 20, 2026

The invoice arrived with a single line that explained the whole case: 412 gigabytes, hosted for fourteen months. The processing was $8,200. The hosting was $61,800. The review was $310,000. The client had budgeted for the review and forgotten the hosting, because hosting is the line item that bills every month the matter stays open — including the months it sits dormant, waiting for a judge, a settlement conference, or a decision nobody has made yet. Discovery is the largest single share of most litigation budgets, and it is the share general counsel understand least, because its price is quoted in three different units at once.

The first unit is the gigabyte. Processing — ingesting data, deduplicating it, extracting text and metadata, indexing it for search — is priced per gigabyte, once. The market rate in 2026 runs $3 to $10 per gigabyte at the vendor level, according to published vendor pricing compiled by Kenodo, and the EDRM and ComplexDiscovery Summer 2025 pricing survey of 70 industry professionals found 43 percent of respondents price initial processing under $25 per gigabyte, with complete processing under $100 per gigabyte for 44 percent. The gap between those two ranges is the gap between a vendor's rate card and a law firm's invoice, and it is the first place a budget goes wrong: the client is quoted the processing rate and billed the service-provider rate.

The second unit is the gigabyte per month. Hosting is where the money accumulates, because it is charged for as long as the data sits in the review platform. The Summer 2025 survey found 56 percent of respondents price hosting under $10 per gigabyte per month without analytics, and under $15 with analytics. The vendor list prices are higher: Everlaw's federal GSA schedule lists $25.24 per gigabyte per month for active data and $3.50 for suspended data. DISCO's public order form with the City of Santa Ana lists Active Review at $40 per gigabyte per month, negotiated down to $25, with Vault storage at $5 — and Houston budgets $278,400 a year for its DISCO software. RelativityOne, on the federal GSA list, runs about $7.28 per gigabyte per month at a 3-terabyte subscription, falling to about $3.71 at 150 terabytes. A 412-gigabyte matter hosted for fourteen months at $10 per gigabyte costs $57,680. At $25, it costs $144,200. The rate is the budget.

The third unit is the document, and it is where the bill is biggest. Review accounts for 60 to 80 percent of total eDiscovery spend in many matters, according to the Summer 2025 survey, and the RAND Institute's study of large companies put the median eDiscovery cost at $1.8 million, with 73 percent of it review. Human review is priced by the hour or by the document: managed review runs $25 to $40 an hour for remote reviewers, according to the survey, with project management at $100 to $200 an hour and forensic collection at $250 to $350 an hour. Contract review is typically priced $1 to $3 per document for first-pass review and $4 to $8 for privilege review, on 2026 vendor pricing.

Where AI actually cuts the bill

Generative AI has changed the review line, and the change is measurable. The Winter 2026 eDiscovery pricing survey from ComplexDiscovery and the EDRM, covering 53 practitioners, found AI first-pass review most often priced at $0.11 to $0.50 per document — below the $0.50 to $1.00 most buyers pay for human first-pass review. Relativity includes its aiR for Review, aiR for Privilege, and aiR for Case Strategy in committed data volume, so the AI line is zero for clients on a committed RelativityOne subscription — now sold as Relativity aiR. Everlaw includes single-document AI suggestions in its per-gigabyte rate, though batch coding runs on credits. DISCO bundles its Cecilia AI features in its per-gigabyte price, but Auto Review, the product that codes documents, carries its own per-document charge.

The honest accounting is that AI cuts the first-pass review cost by roughly half to two-thirds on a per-document basis, and it does not cut the rest. It does not cut processing, which is machine work already. It does not cut hosting, which is storage. It does not cut the privilege review that still requires a lawyer's judgment, or the project management that coordinates the review, or the partner time spent on strategy. A matter that was 73 percent review cost before AI is perhaps 55 percent review cost after it — a real saving, and a smaller one than the pitch decks promise. The survey data supports the narrower claim: technology-assisted review is the least standardized area in the Summer 2025 survey, with 39 percent of respondents using alternative pricing models and only 27 percent pricing under $75 per gigabyte, which means the market has not yet agreed on what AI review should cost.

The billing model has not settled either, and the choice of model changes the budget as much as the rates do. In the Summer 2025 survey, per-document billing led at 37 percent of respondents, followed by hybrid approaches at 30 percent, with per-gigabyte at 11 percent — and user licenses, the seats for reviewers and paralegals, most commonly ran $50 to $100 per user per month. Review is the largest share however it is billed: Nextpoint's analysis of the survey data puts review at an estimated 62 percent of eDiscovery task spend in 2025, followed by processing at 21 percent and collection at 17 percent. The practical implication is that the review model deserves the most attention in the budget, and the hosting model the second most — because a matter that runs long pays hosting every month, and a matter with a large review population pays review on every document, and those are the two lines that compound.

There is one more cost that never appears on a vendor quote and always appears on the invoice: the dormant month. Hosting fees keep billing every month a matter stays open, including the months it sits dormant, and a matter that pauses for six months while the parties negotiate pays six months of hosting for data nobody is reviewing. The fix is a dormant-data tier — Everlaw's suspended rate of $3.50 per gigabyte per month, against $25.24 for active data, or DISCO's Vault at $5 — and a discipline of moving inactive data down a tier the moment the review pauses. A 412-gigabyte matter moved to suspended storage saves roughly $8,900 a month at the Everlaw rates, which is more than the processing cost of the entire matter. The general counsel who manages the tiers manages the budget. The one who does not pays rent on an empty room.

The counterargument: the per-gigabyte model is the problem

There is a case that the pricing model itself, not any vendor, is what makes discovery budgets unpredictable. Per-gigabyte pricing rewards the producing party for collecting less and punishes the requesting party for asking for more, which is the opposite of what discovery is for. Hosting fees keep billing every month a matter stays open, including the months it sits dormant, which means the slowest cases are also the most expensive to store. The Summer 2025 survey shows the market experimenting with alternatives — per-document billing led at 37 percent of respondents, hybrid models at 30 percent — precisely because the per-gigabyte model makes the total unknowable until the matter ends.

The counter to that counter is that the alternatives have their own traps. Per-document pricing makes the review bill depend on how many documents the other side produces, which the client does not control. Flat-fee per-matter pricing looks predictable until the data volume doubles mid-case and the vendor re-prices. The model that works best in practice is the one the client understands well enough to monitor: a per-gigabyte hosting rate with a dormant-data tier, a per-document review rate with an AI first pass, and a monthly report showing data volume, documents reviewed, and spend against the phase budget. Discovery will always be the largest share of the litigation budget. The question is whether the general counsel knows it is the largest share before the invoice arrives.
